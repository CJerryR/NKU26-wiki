Nematodes leave chemical traces

Ascarosides are small signal molecules that nematodes release. We follow two of them: ascr#3 and ascr#18.

Free-living nematodes tend to release more ascr#3 than ascr#18; plant-parasitic ones, the reverse. So the ratio may hint at which nematodes are in a field, and we would like to read it early, before the damage shows.

Schematic only, not measured values.

A receptor from a fungus that traps nematodes

We start from GPR2, an ascaroside receptor identified in a nematode-trapping fungus.

Redesign on the computer

With PyMOL, molecular dynamics and docking we model its binding pocket, and redesign it aiming for a tighter fit to ascr#18.

Put to work in yeast

We express the redesigned receptor in yeast and couple it to the yeast’s own signalling pathway, which leads into the nucleus.

Signal in, color out

The design turns the level of one ascaroside around the cell into a yellow pigment, betaxanthin.

From yeast to test strip

Freeze-dried onto a paper strip, the yeast waits in a small reading window for a sample.

A drop of soil

We wash a soil sample from the field. The extract carries the ascarosides that the nematodes living there have left behind.

Yellow means a warning

The extract goes onto the window. The yeast is designed to answer ascr#18 with betaxanthin, so a yellow window points to plant-parasitic nematodes in that soil.

A test strip for the field

With this yeast we are building a test strip for nematode harm hidden in the soil. Packaged as a kit, our hope is a low-cost, quick early warning for farmland.

Where would it help?

From the bench back to the fields: next, the people across China we have worked with.
