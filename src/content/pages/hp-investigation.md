## Taking Project Questions into the Field {#hp-source-168}

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="169"><strong>Summer fieldwork was an important part of our investigation into agricultural nematode challenges. </strong>We connected museum visits, expert consultations, university and iGEM team exchanges, agricultural visits in Tianjin, and soil collection to examine the project through scientific context, professional methods, and farming experience.</p>

## Museum Visits  Learning How Science Reaches the Public {#hp-source-171}

### National Natural History Museum of China  Soil Life in Context {#hp-source-172}

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="173">Our museum visit offered a reference for understanding biodiversity and organizing science communication. For a nematode project, audiences may first encounter these organisms through disease without knowing their different ecological roles. Placing plant-parasitic nematodes within the wider diversity of life helped us choose a more accurate starting point for education.</p>

### China Agricultural Museum  Placing Detection in Agricultural Practice {#hp-source-174}

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="175">This visit brought our discussion from a single technology back to crops, farming practices, and agricultural development. We considered how to explain the relevance of underground disease to food and vegetable production and how to connect a testing concept with specific steps in crop management.</p>

### Beijing Academy of Agriculture and Forestry Sciences  Communicating Research {#hp-source-176}

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="177">The science outreach facilities offered an opportunity to observe how agricultural research is displayed and explained. The visit helped us understand agricultural technology applications and consider how images, processes, and familiar settings could make our project accessible to people with different backgrounds.</p>

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="178">These visits informed the narratives and communication approaches described on the Education page. Here we present the visits themselves; Education explains how the courses were designed and delivered.</p>

## Expert Interviews  Three Professional Perspectives {#hp-source-185}

### Yurong Yao  Institute of Plant Protection at Tianjin Academy of Agricultural Sciences {#hp-source-186}

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="187">On June 6, 2026, we interviewed Yurong Yao in person at the Institute of Plant Protection, Tianjin Academy of Agricultural Sciences. Starting with nematode disease in protected vegetable production, we discussed hidden underground damage, symptoms that resemble other problems, continuous cropping, and the operational and cost barriers to testing in agricultural practice.</p>

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="188">On sampling, Yao emphasized differences between locations and greenhouses and described practical requirements for transport, storage, separate handling, and post-test disposal. For a potential product, she stressed affordability, ease of operation, and an intuitive readout.</p>

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="189">This discussion brought sampling and the user workflow into our project considerations. It also helped us ask more specific questions in later surveys and farm visits: who would use a test, which procedures would be acceptable, and how could results inform agricultural management?</p>

### Qian Liu  College of Plant Protection at China Agricultural University {#hp-source-192}

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="193">On August 18, Qian Liu discussed nematode identification, early detection, and validation with real samples. She explained the roles of morphological and molecular methods, stressed that identification claims must match their purpose and validation, and connected early detection with management decisions before planting.</p>

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="194">For our ascaroside-based approach, she advised establishing whether signal differences could support the proposed inference and distinguishing compound detection from conclusions about nematode species or density. We also discussed sample representativeness, extraction losses, matrix interference, controls, and staged validation.</p>

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="195">Liu also drew our attention to total testing cost, sample preparation, storage, and operational demands. Her advice helped us examine a field-oriented prototype from the perspective of diagnostic practice, beyond the speed of the final reaction.</p>

### Zhihao Pang  Beijing Forestry University {#hp-source-198}

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="199">During our online discussion on August 18, Zhihao Pang examined the project through soil processes and potential use. He asked whether soil background color could affect interpretation and whether different degradation rates of ascr#3 and ascr#18 could change their measured relative composition.</p>

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="200">He also suggested obtaining observations from genuinely affected fields, speaking with potential users, and comparing portability and simpler procedures with existing sample-submission services. For engineered yeast, he raised concerns about environmental release and post-test handling.</p>

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="201">This exchange identified alternative explanations that we could examine: could the same color arise from different backgrounds, could degradation alter a measured ratio, and could a user complete the workflow? The connections between these questions and our wet-lab and dry-lab work are developed on the iHP page.</p>

## iGEM Team Exchanges: Sharing Research and Human Practices Experience {#hp-source-204}

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="205"><strong>This season, we exchanged ideas with fellow iGEM teams to explore experimental approaches, discuss shared challenges, and learn from one another’s Human Practices experience.</strong> These exchanges connected us with teams from Zhengzhou University, Peking University, Tsinghua University, China Agricultural University, and the University of Science and Technology of China, as well as the PROFUTURE team.</p>

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="206">Discussions of <strong>project design and research approaches </strong>covered target and receptor selection, receptor engineering, experimental strategies, and modeling. With Peking University, we discussed potential applications, receptor modification, and how to communicate the project’s rationale. With Tsinghua University, we explored coordination between wet-lab and dry-lab work alongside HP organization. With China Agricultural University, we compared yeast-based sensing approaches and discussed experimental challenges and potential user needs.</p>

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="207">Exchanges on Human Practices and education offered practical perspectives on planning interviews, distributing questionnaires, building educational partnerships, and communicating with the public. We met the Zhengzhou University team online on July 10 and the PROFUTURE team online on August 6. We shared our experience of developing lessons and delivering outreach through the Xiangyang Program, while learning how other teams organized their own engagement activities.</p>

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="208">We met the Peking University and Tsinghua University teams on August 17 and the China Agricultural University team on August 18. A later exchange with the University of Science and Technology of China team provided another opportunity to share project and HP experience. <strong>Together, these conversations gave us additional perspectives for reviewing our research plans and organizing our engagement activities.</strong></p>

## Farm Visits and Soil Collection in Tianjin {#hp-source-216}

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="217"><strong>On August 20, we visited Xiangyu Fruit and Vegetable Farm and the vegetable demonstration farm in Diliubu Village, Xiqing District, Tianjin. </strong>Guided by Professor Qilin Yu, team members took part in farm work and explored disease management, soil microbial products, and production practices in context.</p>

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="218">At Diliubu Village, discussion with a grower moved from visible leaf pests to roots and underground disease. The conversation showed how needs vary locally: nematodes are not a leading current concern in every field, but when an outbreak occurs, management may involve soil disinfection followed by efforts to restore microbial communities.</p>

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="219">This reminded us to discuss testing needs in relation to particular crops and locations. Listening to growers’ experience, alongside professional methods for identifying causes, helps keep the project connected with real agricultural problems.</p>

<p data-hp-source-paragraph="220">The team also organized soil collection and produced a sampling guidance video. Sample identifiers, crop, location, date, and treatment history connect contributors with subsequent research. The guidance explains which information to retain and how to support sample collection.</p>
