We do not want future teams to start from zero.

We want them to start from where we stopped—and go further.

## Wet Lab {#wet-lab toc="Wet Lab"}

Our Wet Lab work supports not only the construction and validation of the yeast biosensor in this project. More importantly, we intend to make the genetic parts, engineered strains, plasmids, donor DNA, experimental protocols and practical experience generated during our research available as public resources for future iGEM teams to reference, reuse and extend.

These contributions allow subsequent teams to build on our established resources and experience, rather than constructing comparable yeast engineering systems entirely from scratch.

### Parts and Collections {#parts-and-collections toc="Parts and Collections"}

In this project, we have submitted 39 Parts and 4 Collections to the iGEM Registry ([https://registry.igem.org/organisations/igem/6303](https://registry.igem.org/organisations/igem/6303)). These submissions cover multiple modules including yeast chassis construction, genome editing, tool plasmids, sgRNA, receptor expression and functional components of biosensors.

Among all submitted parts, we designed and constructed 10 plasmids (Table 1). These plasmids serve as core components of our project and also provide direct references for future teams undertaking similar yeast strain construction work.

Meanwhile, we have also established the following four Collections with clearly defined functional roles:

#### Chassis Strain Construction Collection {#chassis-strain-construction-collection toc="Chassis Strain Construction Collection"}

This Collection contains key genetic parts for the engineering construction of yeast biosensor chassis, including parts related to chassis modification and functional module integration.

With this Collection, we aim to provide a clear resource entry for future teams working on the engineering modification of *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, allowing them to conveniently reference our chassis construction strategy.

#### Tool Plasmid Family {#tool-plasmid-family toc="Tool Plasmid Family"}

This Collection systematically organizes the tool plasmids and associated genetic parts utilized in this project.

By centrally assembling these scattered tool components, we aim to reduce the effort required for future teams to locate similar tools, and enable these resources to be readily recombined for diverse yeast engineering projects.

#### sgRNA Family {#sgrna-family toc="sgRNA Family"}

This Collection contains the sgRNA genes used in this project, providing sequence and design references for future teams conducting similar yeast genome editing work.

#### GPR2 variant family {#gpr2-variant-family toc="GPR2 variant family"}

This Collection contains the cDNA sequences of the key Gpr2 receptor and its mutants utilized in our project.

These parts serve as essential components for constructing our yeast GPCR sensing system. They also hold potential for further applications in receptor engineering, ligand response research and the development of yeast biosensors.

In summary, beyond submitting individual Parts, we have built Collections grouped by function and application scenarios. This allows future teams to rapidly locate the core components required for their projects.

### Experimental Methods and Practical Experience {#experimental-methods-and-practical-experience toc="Experimental Methods and Practical Experience"}

During project implementation, we systematically compiled the major Wet Lab methods involved in this project (Wet Lab-Experiments). We believe that Wet Lab Contribution should not only demonstrate successful experimental results. So we documented not only standard experimental protocols but also the practical experience accumulated throughout our experiments.

During practical experiments, we also encountered difficulties including problematic PCR amplification, fluctuating yeast transformation efficiency and failed one-step cloning. Therefore, we compiled these experiences into Troubleshooting & Lessons Learned, aiming to convert the "cost of failures" into readily usable experience for future teams.

## Dry Lab {#dry-lab toc="Dry Lab"}

### Docking: Engineering for Two Ligands and Cross-Receptor Residue Analysis {#docking-engineering-for-two-ligands-and-cross-receptor-residue-analysis toc="Docking: Engineering for Two Ligands and Cross-Receptor Residue Analysis"}

#### Receptor Engineering Guided by Differences in Contacts with Multiple Ligands {#receptor-engineering-guided-by-differences-in-contacts-with-multiple-ligands toc="Receptor Engineering Guided by Differences in Contacts with Multiple Ligands"}

To investigate the differential recognition of ascr#18 and ascr#3 by Gpr2, we developed an engineering workflow combining contact analysis for both ligands, side-chain substitutions, and conformational comparisons. Substitution sites were selected from the contact distributions of the wild-type (WT) receptor with the two ligands. Changes in docking scores after substitution were then examined alongside changes in ligand position, contacting residues, and interaction types to assess how side-chain modifications affected the two ligands differently. This workflow was applied to single-mutant screening and exploration of mutation combinations, with subsequent work continuing to focus on single-site engineering.

#### Cross-Receptor Residue Analysis Integrating Contact Bias and Positional Correspondence {#cross-receptor-residue-analysis-integrating-contact-bias-and-positional-correspondence toc="Cross-Receptor Residue Analysis Integrating Contact Bias and Positional Correspondence"}

Drawing on the receptor structure comparisons reported by Hu et al. [B03], we included three fungal receptors, Gpr2, Gpr3, and GprC, together with DAF-37 and SRBC-64 from *Caenorhabditis elegans*, in an analysis of contacts with both ligands and residue correspondence. Five predicted models were used for each receptor. Contact frequencies for the two ligands were paired within each model before contact biases were summarized, and positional correspondence was examined through sequence and structural mapping.

Residue annotations recorded contact bias, positional correspondence, and construct status. The positions corresponding to some contacting residues in GprC fell within deleted regions of Gpr2 and Gpr3 and were therefore marked as absent from the constructs.

The tryptophan positions in transmembrane helix 6 (TM6) of the three fungal receptors—Gpr2 W338 (BW: to be verified), Gpr3 W306 (BW: to be verified), and GprC W388 (BW: to be verified)—all showed contact differences favoring ascr#18, with relatively consistent support from sequence and structural mapping. In Ballesteros–Weinstein (BW) numbering, the first number identifies the transmembrane helix, and the second indicates the position relative to a conserved reference residue within that helix, which is assigned the number 50. [BW01]

For DAF-37 and SRBC-64, sequence alignment and structural mapping identified different residues. GprC N56 showed a contact difference within the receptor: both ligands could contact this position, but ascr#3 had a higher contact frequency in some models, while support for cross-receptor correspondence was weaker. These observations led us to propose parallel substitutions at corresponding positions in the fungal receptors and to identify questions for testing receptor-specific contact sites. Detailed results are presented in the residue correspondence and receptor-specific contact analysis under Model/Docking.

#### Tools for Checking Docking Inputs and Exporting Poses {#tools-for-checking-docking-inputs-and-exporting-poses toc="Tools for Checking Docking Inputs and Exporting Poses"}

Predicted receptor orientation, rotations applied during membrane construction, and segment deletion can alter the search region. We used structural superposition to establish a common coordinate reference for the receptor, ligand, and search box. The membrane normal after membrane construction and the segments actually retained were used to check whether poses were located on the intracellular or extracellular side and whether truncation had exposed cavities. For Gpr3, extracellular local searches used extracellular loop 2 (ECL2) as a positional prior.

The loss of formal charges during ligand format conversion affected the identification of charged interactions. We developed an atom-mapping verification tool to compare atoms, coordinates, and corresponding structural information between the molecular structure file in SDF format and the docking file in PDBQT format. A pose-export tool used the complete Meeko structural metadata retained in the PDBQT file to reconstruct pose SDF files for subsequent contact analysis. [B05, B06]

### MD: Joint Analysis of Receptor Motion and Local Ligand Behavior {#md-joint-analysis-of-receptor-motion-and-local-ligand-behavior toc="MD: Joint Analysis of Receptor Motion and Local Ligand Behavior"}

In molecular dynamics (MD) analysis, we combined receptor backbone root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), relative ligand position, and local contacts to track receptor–ligand relationships during conformational changes. For Gpr3, a local residue cluster was defined using the docking interaction fingerprint (IFP). We calculated the distance between the ligand and this region and tracked the initial contacts and specific hydrogen bonds.

In one 200 ns trajectory of Gpr3 R167Y–ascr#3, the receptor backbone shifted substantially from its initial structure after 100 ns, while the ligand remained close to the residue cluster and retained some of its initial contacts. Residue-level contact between ARG175 and the ligand persisted across all analyzed frames of the trajectory, whereas the specific hydrogen bond examined formed in only some frames. See “Gpr3 R167Y: Coexistence of Global Displacement and Local Contacts” under Model/MD.

### ODE Model {#ode-model toc="ODE Model"}

#### ODE: A Modular Modeling Approach for Limited Experimental Data {#ode-a-modular-modeling-approach-for-limited-experimental-data toc="ODE: A Modular Modeling Approach for Limited Experimental Data"}

For biosensors with multiple stages of signal transduction, a central modeling challenge is to retain the key mechanisms while ensuring that the model can be constrained by available experimental data. Using our yeast GPCR biosensing system, we developed a modeling approach that moves from mapping the pathway and selecting mathematical representations to simplifying individual modules. This approach offers a reference for teams working with similar signaling pathways.

We first organized the process from ligand recognition to colorimetric output into seven nodes: ligand input L, receptor activity R_active, G protein G(t), MAPK M(t), synthetic transcription factor activity TF, GAL product formation rate Prod, and colorimetric signal C(t). Drawing on mechanistic descriptions and timescale information from the literature, we used ordinary differential equations in the initial model to describe the dynamics of G protein, MAPK, and the colorimetric signal. Hill-type or other saturating algebraic relationships linked receptor responses, transcriptional regulation, and output generation. This structure yielded a normalized prototype with three dynamic states and made explicit the conditions under which each simplifying approximation applied.

We then reassessed model complexity against the measurements available from wet-lab experiments. When experiments primarily measure the final colorimetric output, the gains and thresholds of individual intermediate steps are often difficult to determine separately. We therefore combined the original output equations to derive a single MAPK-driven colorimetric equation encompassing sTF, GAL production rate, and color accumulation. With a fixed copy number and under the assumptions of the original model, this reduction preserved the original input-output relationship while reducing the number of parameters that needed to be determined separately. The derivation and equivalence checks have been completed, and integration with the main model is underway.

For the intermediate signaling modules, we independently ran and checked published models to assess how well their reaction structures matched our project pathway. For components that still require project-specific data, we retained provisions for parameter calibration and mapped each parameter to the experimental measurements needed to determine it. This organization allows the model to evolve as construct information and experimental data become available.

Other teams can start from our example model and use the equation descriptions and code to replace the receptor, signaling, or reporter modules with those of their own systems. For teams with limited experimental data, we demonstrate how to combine intermediate steps that are difficult to measure separately and explain which parameters can be informed by the literature and which require their own experiments. The accompanying literature comparisons and run records can also help users select suitable published models and identify what needs to be checked when transferring them to a new system. Together, these resources aim to help other teams move from a pathway diagram to a model that can be run, interpreted, and updated with experimental data.

### Economic Benefit Model {#economic-benefit-model toc="Economic Benefit Model"}

To help other teams assess whether a detection technology is worth using in practice, we developed a reusable framework for analyzing the economic benefits of early detection. The framework can be applied across sensor types. It connects pathogen or pest density, crop yield loss, product prices, testing costs, treatment costs, and treatment efficacy to compare management scenarios, including no testing and no treatment, uniform treatment without testing, and risk-based treatment following testing.

The framework first uses the relationship between density and yield to estimate relative yield and economic losses at different infestation levels. It then calculates the losses that management could prevent, the remaining losses, treatment costs, and expected net benefits. Users can apply the same calculation structure to new settings by supplying a density-yield relationship appropriate to the local crop and target organism, together with local prices and management costs.

We also provide an example involving greenhouse tomatoes in Spain and the southern root-knot nematode. It demonstrates how a yield-loss model fitted using published data, a three-tier risk classification, and per-unit-area cost calculations can be combined into a complete decision report. Model parameters are kept separate from report content, allowing users to replace crop prices, testing costs, treatment costs, risk thresholds, and treatment efficacy without redesigning the entire analysis workflow.

Reusability also requires restraint when evidence is insufficient. If risk thresholds are available for a crop but a reliable density-yield relationship is missing, the model provides a risk indication without proceeding to calculate monetary losses or return on investment. When detection performance, sampling error, or treatment efficacy has yet to be validated, these parameters remain subject to calibration rather than being presented as firm commercial conclusions.
