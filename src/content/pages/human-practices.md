## Listening Changed the Questions We Asked {#ihp-source-73 toc="Listening Changed the Questions We Asked"}

NemaKlear began with a technical question: could engineered yeast respond to nematode-associated chemical signals and produce a visible output?

Human Practices expanded that question. Which agricultural situation would justify screening? How would a user collect a meaningful sample? What could a positive result establish? How might a negative result mislead? What information would be needed before the system could support a real agricultural decision?

We investigated these questions through public questionnaires, agricultural visits, expert discussions, exchanges with other iGEM teams, educational activities, and industry conversations. Each method contributed a different kind of evidence.

Our approach follows a continuing sequence: listen, interpret, decide, revise, and evaluate. We distinguish a change in our priorities or documentation from a demonstrated improvement in sensor performance.

## Choosing Whose Perspectives to Include {#ihp-source-78 toc="Choosing Whose Perspectives to Include"}

Growers and agricultural practitioners helped us understand observation, sampling, and crop-management decisions. Plant-protection and academic specialists provided perspectives on biological interpretation, candidate signals, controls, and validation.

Other iGEM teams offered opportunities to compare research approaches and Human Practices methods. We exchanged ideas with teams from Zhengzhou University, Peking University, Tsinghua University, China Agricultural University, and the University of Science and Technology of China.

Industry participants helped us consider technical development, partnership expectations, and the practical conditions of future translation. Students and members of the public revealed questions that scientific explanations can overlook, including what a result means and why uncertainty matters.

These groups were selected for their relationship to the problem. Their perspectives are complementary, but they are not interchangeable: public opinion cannot establish analytical specificity, and an expert's assessment cannot substitute for experimental data.

## Questionnaires From Laboratory Questions to Public Responses {#ihp-source-83 toc="Questionnaires From Laboratory Questions to Public Responses"}

Our agricultural nematode awareness and needs questionnaire developed alongside the project. Early laboratory ideas informed questions about underground pest risks, testing expectations, and result interpretation. Responses then provided material for discussing subsequent research priorities.

The questionnaire was circulated through team communication channels and the Xiangyang teaching-support network. This broadened participation while also introducing sampling limitations. The responses describe the people who participated, rather than a statistically representative population of agricultural users.

The survey helped us recognize that explaining a screening result is part of the design problem. A useful output must communicate both its possible significance and its limits. Direct user research and technical calibration remain necessary before a proposed output can become a validated decision aid.

## Agricultural Visits Understanding the Whole Workflow {#ihp-source-87 toc="Agricultural Visits Understanding the Whole Workflow"}

On August 20, we visited Xiangyu Fruit and Vegetable Garden and the Diliubu Village premium vegetable demonstration site in Xiqing District, Tianjin.

Discussions addressed visible crop problems, root and soil conditions, pest management, and the recovery of soil ecosystems after treatment. We treated these observations as context for understanding agricultural decisions, without assuming that every observed symptom was caused by nematodes.

The visit broadened our understanding of usability. “Easy to use” must include sampling location, timing, preparation, controls, result interpretation, and access to follow-up advice. It cannot be reduced to the simplicity of a color readout.

Soil collection and our sampling guidance video extended this work by retaining sample context for subsequent research. Sample collection itself is not evidence of field detection performance.

## Integration Case 1 Soil Complexity and the Limits of Specificity {#ihp-source-92 toc="Integration Case 1 Soil Complexity and the Limits of Specificity"}

### Feedback {#ihp-source-93 toc="Feedback"}

Scientific and industry discussions raised concerns about mixed nematode populations, overlapping chemical signals, soil background effects, and whether a proposed warning would occur early enough to inform action.

### Decision {#ihp-source-95 toc="Decision"}

We identified matrix effects and biological specificity as separate questions from signal generation. A response in a simplified system would not, by itself, establish usefulness in agricultural soil.

### Project Change {#ihp-source-97 toc="Project Change"}

This perspective shaped our description of NemaKlear as a screening concept. It also helped define questions for dry- and wet-laboratory work: which candidate inputs should be compared, which controls are needed, and how background responses should be distinguished from target-associated responses.

We incorporated these limitations into public explanations and industry-facing discussions so that the project's presentation would remain consistent with its evidence.

### Verification and Next Step {#ihp-source-100 toc="Verification and Next Step"}

A clearer scope and a better-defined testing question are outcomes of this integration. Demonstrating improved soil performance requires separate experimental evidence. Modeling can support hypothesis development and test selection, but it cannot establish field specificity.

The next validation stage should examine relevant soil extracts, interfering signals, appropriate negative controls, recovery, and comparison with reference measurements.

## Integration Case 2 User Needs and Readable Results {#ihp-source-103 toc="Integration Case 2 User Needs and Readable Results"}

### Feedback {#ihp-source-104 toc="Feedback"}

Agricultural engagement emphasized manageable procedures and results that users can understand without specialist training.

### Decision {#ihp-source-106 toc="Decision"}

We treated accessibility as a property of the complete workflow. We also separated a proposed visual output from a validated risk classification.

### Project Change {#ihp-source-108 toc="Project Change"}

Sampling guidance, result explanations, and the color-card concept became linked design considerations. Our educational explanations were adjusted to clarify the difference between detecting a signal, identifying a nematode, and choosing a management response.

A future interpretation guide should explain valid, invalid, positive, negative, and uncertain outcomes. Any risk categories must be supported by calibration rather than visual appearance alone.

### Verification and Next Step {#ihp-source-111 toc="Verification and Next Step"}

The color-card concept expresses a user-oriented design direction. Establishing usability requires testing whether intended users can follow instructions, recognize an invalid test, interpret uncertainty, and identify an appropriate next action.

We therefore identify user evaluation and reference-method comparison as necessary next steps before claiming field usability or reliable risk classification.

## Integration Case 3 Partnership Expectations and Responsible Commitments {#ihp-source-114 toc="Integration Case 3 Partnership Expectations and Responsible Commitments"}

### Feedback {#ihp-source-115 toc="Feedback"}

Industry conversations highlighted the need for clear sponsorship deliverables and fulfillment conditions. Different organizations also expressed different interests, including research exchange, student development, public engagement, and corporate social responsibility.

### Decision {#ihp-source-117 toc="Decision"}

We separated support for team activities from scientific validation and competition results. Partnership commitments must remain within the team's capacity and institutional authorization.

### Project Change {#ihp-source-119 toc="Project Change"}

Our partnership planning distinguishes possible acknowledgements, educational collaboration, and other deliverables from uncertain outcomes such as awards or future commercialization.

Proposed benefits require a defined scope, responsible person, delivery conditions, and appropriate authorization. Talent-related opportunities should be voluntary, with no disclosure of personal information without consent. Future intellectual-property or commercial arrangements require the relevant institutional process.

### Verification and Next Step {#ihp-source-122 toc="Verification and Next Step"}

The appropriate evidence for this integration is a documented agreement and a record of delivery. A proposal or conversation alone does not demonstrate that a partnership has been completed.

Any jointly organized educational activity remains a planned activity until implementation is documented. Financial or material support does not determine our interpretation of experimental findings.

## Responsibility Safety and Environmental Considerations {#ihp-source-125 toc="Responsibility Safety and Environmental Considerations"}

We considered how a screening system could cause harm as well as offer benefits. False reassurance could delay professional diagnosis; false positives could contribute to unnecessary treatment. Our intended use therefore includes confirmation and contextual interpretation rather than automatic treatment recommendations.

Containment, handling, and disposal are central to the yeast-based concept. Freeze-drying is a preservation method, not evidence of inactivation. Any future field-facing format requires appropriate containment, risk assessment, authorization, and waste-management procedures.

We also considered accessibility. A low reagent cost would not guarantee an affordable workflow if sampling, transport, training, or storage remained burdensome.

For questionnaires, interviews, classroom activities, and public documentation, participant privacy and appropriate consent are essential. Particular care is required when publishing images or work involving minors. Sponsorship and collaboration should be acknowledged transparently while preserving scientific independence.

## What Human Practices Changed {#ihp-source-130 toc="What Human Practices Changed"}

Human Practices changed how we define the project's task. Our focus expanded from producing a visible response to asking whether the sampling process, signal interpretation, containment, and follow-up pathway could support responsible use.

It also changed how we communicate progress. Scientific evidence, stakeholder advice, educational feedback, and partnership support answer different questions. Reporting them separately makes the project easier to evaluate and provides a clearer basis for the next development stage.
