<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="2">Why do plants become ill? What lives unseen in soil? How do living things sense changes around them? These questions are our starting point for introducing the project. Drawing on agricultural nematodes and early warning, we translated the idea of signal input, biological response, and visible output into courses, picture books, and outreach materials. Learners begin with familiar plants and soil before exploring the principles of synthetic biology.</p>

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="6">Education also helps us examine how we explain our work. For learners of different ages, we need to make clear how nematodes relate to agriculture, how the sensor works, and what a color change can tell us. This encourages us to revisit our terminology, illustrations, and explanations, and consider how to make the testing process and the meaning of its results easier to understand.</p>

::::

## Three courses form a learning pathway {#education-source-4}

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="8">We designed three courses around agricultural problems, biological signals, and scientific responsibility. Each connects nematodes, agriculture, and synthetic biology from a different angle, inviting learners to observe, propose explanations, and consider how science relates to everyday life.</p>

### 1.1 Science course: The Nematode Hunt {#education-source-6}

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="10">The Nematode Hunt introduces nematodes and their relationships with plants and agriculture. Beginning with familiar crops and signs of poor growth, the course helps learners connect above-ground observations with roots and soil, and understand why underground disease can be difficult to notice.</p>

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="11">The course also introduces the diversity of soil life and the different ecological roles of nematodes. Alongside the damage caused by plant-parasitic nematodes, learners explore the different relationships between organisms and crops.</p>

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="12">We want learners to move from noticing a problem to asking what might explain it: what could make a plant grow poorly, and what else would we need to observe or find out? These questions also reflect the agricultural problem behind our project: a test should provide clues that help people decide what to investigate next.</p>

### 1.2 Experimental science course: Exploring Biological Signals {#education-source-10}

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="14">Exploring Biological Signals follows the sequence of input, sensing, and output. Through age-appropriate activities, learners explore how living systems sense signals and how an invisible change can become something observable.</p>

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="15">The course connects with our engineered-yeast sensor concept by introducing the sensing element, signal transmission, and visible output step by step. As learners observe results and propose explanations, they also have opportunities to consider experimental conditions and controls: what comparisons would help us understand where a change came from?</p>

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="16">This course focuses on understanding the method. For our team, turning the sensor concept into a lesson requires us to explain what each part does and how the parts work together. Practical materials and procedures follow the lesson plan for each age group.</p>

### 1.3 Values and responsibility course: Small Lives and Everyday Contributions {#education-source-14}

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="18">Small Lives and Everyday Contributions connects microscopic life with careful observation, cooperation, and everyday contributions. It invites learners to notice work in research and agricultural service that often goes unseen.</p>

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="19">The course extends scientific themes into discussions of responsibility and personal growth, including respect for life, persistence in inquiry, and contributing to shared needs. Familiar experiences provide a way to ask how a small observation or action can matter to someone else’s life.</p>

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="20">This also reflects why we began the project: to study biological systems while understanding agricultural needs and the responsibilities involved in explaining and applying research.</p>

## Adapting the same theme for different ages {#education-source-21}

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="27">The science and experimental science courses each have primary-school and middle-school versions. The values and responsibility course is designed for primary-school learners. We adjust learning goals, depth of explanation, illustrations, and activities to suit learners’ existing knowledge and classroom conditions.</p>

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="31">Designing for different ages means deciding what we most want learners to take away from a lesson: discovering a form of life below ground, understanding how a signal can be sensed, or learning to ask another question about a result. It also helps us organize our project explanations, beginning with the problem and basic principles before introducing more complex conditions and interpretations.</p>

## Bringing the courses into classrooms through teaching partnerships {#education-source-27}

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="33">We delivered courses and outreach activities through Nankai University’s Xiangyang Program public-interest education service team, mainly in May, July, and late August 2026. Nematode-themed lessons and supporting materials reached classrooms or communities in Gansu, Hebei, Jiangxi, and Tianjin. Team members and teaching partners delivered sessions according to local arrangements.</p>

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="34">Our team designed the courses and supporting materials and worked with teaching partners on age suitability, preparation, and activity sequences. The existing education network brought the courses into different settings, while partners’ classroom experience offered input for future revisions.</p>

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="40">The partnership also drew our attention to the practical conditions of teaching: were the materials suitable for the age group, was the explanation easy to follow, and could the activities be prepared readily? Courses and questionnaires reached people with different backgrounds through shared channels, providing material about learning and public understanding respectively. ## Using feedback to improve future teaching {#education-source-35}

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="43">Educational feedback needs to return to specific learning goals. Naming a nematode and explaining why similar plant symptoms require further investigation reflect different levels of understanding. For the sensor topic, we also want to consider whether learners can describe the connections between input, sensing, and output.</p>

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="44">When organizing our teaching materials, we use worksheets, classroom records, and learner work to understand the learning process, while paying attention to requests for experiments and interaction. These materials help us consider how to assess whether a lesson has explained its central ideas and what to focus on next time.</p>

### 4.1 What the available feedback suggests {#education-source-38}

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="46">The Xiangyang Program’s summer teaching evaluation report described interest in more experiments, interaction, and age-appropriate courses. It provides input for teaching improvements across the partnership. Understanding learning in our nematode courses requires records and learner work from the relevant sessions.</p>

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="47">This feedback reminds us to consider how learners participate alongside the scientific content: do the illustrations help, do activities give learners opportunities to observe and explain, and does the level suit what they already know? For our project, clearly explaining input, sensing, and output, while leaving room for questions and comparisons, remains a useful focus.</p>

### 4.2 Gathering more specific feedback next time {#education-source-41}

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="49">Future sessions could collect feedback through a small number of tasks tied to the learning goals. Learners might explain several possible causes of a plant’s symptoms, draw how a sensor turns an input into an output, or suggest a comparison that would help explain an observation.</p>

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="50">Such tasks could help us record what learners understand and decide how to adjust illustrations, terminology, or activities. In this way, education continues to address a communication question within our project: how can people with different backgrounds understand the sensing principle and the connection between an observation and a risk assessment?</p>

## Beyond the classroom: outreach and reusable resources {#education-source-44}

### 5.1 Explaining the project through different materials {#education-source-45}

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="53">Picture books, posters, leaflets, postcards, fieldwork reports, and a sampling guidance video present the project’s agricultural questions, biological sensing concept, and sampling requirements in different forms. Images and stories give people outside the classroom ways to encounter life below ground and understand why our team studies agricultural nematodes.</p>

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="56">Illustrated materials such as The Yeast Detective and the Secret Signals in Soil provide a visual introduction to engineered yeast and soil signals. The Yeast Detective’s Three Steps to Helping Farmers connects the project with an agricultural setting. The sampling guidance video links field procedures with the background information contributors need to retain; the Investigation page explains this workflow in more details.</p>

These choices help us examine our project explanations from the audience’s perspective.</p>

### 5.2 Helping other educators use the courses {#education-source-52}

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="63">The three courses and their age-adapted versions provide examples of learning goals, preparation, activity sequences, and feedback methods. Educators can adapt them to learners’ ages, locally familiar crops, and classroom conditions, using agricultural nematodes as a starting point for observing the surrounding environment and discussing synthetic biology.</p>

<p data-hp-draft-paragraph="64">When adapting a course, educators can first identify the central question they want learners to understand, then select a suitable version and supporting illustrations. Short tasks, questions, and learner work can help document learning and provide feedback for the next session.</p>
