## From Soil to Shared Scientific Questions {#education-from-soil-to-shared-scientific-questions toc="Shared questions"}

<div id="listening-to-the-context" class="content-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></div>

<div id="from-hidden-soil-risks-to-shared-scientific-learning" class="content-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></div>

Our education work begins with an everyday question: what might be happening below the soil when a plant looks unhealthy?

This question connects plant-parasitic nematodes with food production, biological signaling, and scientific investigation. It also creates space for uncertainty: similar symptoms can have different causes, and a proposed sensor requires evidence before it can guide decisions.

Our aim is to support **scientific learning and dialogue**. We invite learners to ask questions, suggest explanations, and examine the limits of a technology—including our own.

## Designing for Different Learners {#education-designing-for-different-learners toc="Course themes"}

<div id="values-oriented-course-small-lives-bright-light-growing-through-ordinary-perseverance" class="content-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></div>

<div id="experimental-science-course-the-signal-exploration-challenge" class="content-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></div>

<div id="theoretical-science-course-the-nematode-chase" class="content-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></div>

<div id="three-course-series" class="content-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></div>

We developed three course themes.

**The Great Nematode Chase** introduces nematodes, their diversity, and their relationship with plants and agriculture. It helps learners connect visible plant symptoms with possible underground causes without assuming that all nematodes are harmful.

**The Signal Discovery Challenge** introduces the relationship between a biological signal, a sensing system, and an observable output. It encourages learners to consider controls and alternative explanations. Its practical activities should be understood according to the materials used in each lesson; the course title does not imply the handling of engineered organisms.

**Small Lives, Shared Responsibility** connects curiosity about small organisms with scientific inquiry, cooperation, and responsibility. It encourages learners to discuss how careful observation and ordinary contributions can support shared goals.

The theoretical and experimental themes include primary-school and junior-secondary versions. The responsibility-focused course is designed for primary-school learners. These are age-adapted versions of three course themes, rather than additional independent teaching events.

## Classroom Implementation Through Teaching Partnerships {#education-classroom-implementation-through-teaching-partnerships toc="Teaching partnerships"}

<div id="bringing-the-courses-to-learners" class="content-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></div>

Through partnerships including Nankai University's Xiangyang teaching-support initiative, our educational materials reached classroom and community settings in Gansu, Hebei, Jiangxi, and Tianjin.

The team contributed nematode-themed course design and supporting materials. Delivery involved team members and teaching partners according to individual activity arrangements.

Our classroom approach uses agricultural examples, illustrations, questions, and age-appropriate activities to connect unfamiliar concepts with learners' experiences. Activity sheets and creative tasks provide opportunities to express ideas in ways that do not depend entirely on technical vocabulary.

The learning goals are to recognize possible underground causes of plant problems, understand the basic **input–response–output idea of a biosensor**, and **distinguish a screening clue from a confirmed diagnosis**.

## Dialogue and Learning in Both Directions {#education-dialogue-and-learning-in-both-directions toc="Two-way learning"}

Learners' questions help us assess the clarity of our explanations. Can a color change tell us exactly which organism is present? Are all nematodes harmful? What would happen if a sample contained several different signals?

These questions are also relevant to our research. They prompt us to explain **controls, specificity, and uncertainty** more clearly.

Questions, activity sheets, and learner-created work can provide direct evidence of how a lesson is understood. However, **participation and enthusiasm alone** do not establish a measurable gain in scientific knowledge.

Our next course-evaluation step is to use a small set of lesson-specific questions and interpretation tasks, keeping them separate from wider teaching-program surveys.

## What the Available Feedback Shows {#education-what-the-available-feedback-shows toc="Available feedback"}

The Xiangyang 2026 summer teaching-support program conducted a broader evaluation. [HP4](#education-ref-4)

| Wider program measure | Responses |
|---|---:|
| Valid pre-program questionnaires | 91 |
| Valid post-program questionnaires | 113 |
| Answers to the post-program satisfaction question | 106 |
| “Very satisfied” or “satisfied” | 91 of 106 (85.8%) |

This result concerns the **overall teaching-support program**. It is **not a satisfaction score for our nematode course**.

Open feedback expressed interest in more experiments, interaction, and teaching suited to learners' levels of understanding. These comments support our emphasis on age-appropriate explanations and participatory activities, but they do not establish the effect of an individual lesson.

The pre- and post-program samples were not strictly paired, and the post-program dataset included additional online junior-secondary responses. We therefore do not attribute differences between the groups to our iGEM courses.

## Extending Learning Beyond the Classroom {#education-extending-learning-beyond-the-classroom toc="Public engagement"}

Our educational materials include **illustrated content, leaflets, postcards, posters, practice reports, and a sampling guidance video**. These formats connect classroom learning with public discussion of agriculture and synthetic biology.

Public-facing explanations draw on field visits and research questions, while clearly distinguishing observations, hypotheses, and experimental findings. Questions raised through public engagement help identify terminology and assumptions that need further explanation.

The agricultural awareness questionnaire and the teaching-program evaluation serve different purposes. They are reported separately rather than combined into a single measure of educational impact.

## Industry Connections and Future Educational Activities {#education-industry-connections-and-future-educational-activities toc="Future activities"}

Industry engagement offers opportunities to discuss how agricultural biotechnology develops beyond the laboratory. A university–industry science salon is **a planned extension** of this work, potentially combining a technical talk, a student project discussion, and an open question-and-answer session.

If implemented, the activity should have explicit learning objectives, transparent acknowledgement of support, and opportunities for participants to question both the technology and its proposed use.

Corporate support may help provide materials or access to expertise. Educational content and evaluation must remain grounded in scientific learning and participant needs.

## Resources Others Can Adapt {#education-resources-others-can-adapt toc="Reusable materials"}

<div id="resources-and-next-steps" class="content-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></div>

Our course presentations, illustrated materials, activity sheets, and sampling guidance provide a foundation for future educators and iGEM teams.

Responsible reuse requires the **appropriate age version, clear learning objectives**, preparation instructions, acknowledgement, and adaptation to the local setting. Evaluation should examine what learners understood and what the educators learned from their questions.

By connecting agricultural experience, classroom dialogue, and scientific reflection, we aim to make synthetic biology more understandable and more open to participation.
