## 1. Docking | From two-ligand contact differences to receptor-engineering hypotheses {#docking-introduction toc="1. Docking | From two-ligand contact differences to receptor-engineering hypotheses"}

To build a sensing system that distinguishes ascr#18 from ascr#3, we seek receptor candidates that favor each of the two ligands. Docking helps us identify engineering clues from predicted structures: which residues the two ligands contact, how scores and contacts change after the relevant side chains are altered, and which candidates warrant further study.

Around these questions, we progressively established the comparison conditions, proposed sites from the contact differences in wild type (WT), and adjusted the design through alanine substitutions, side-chain scanning and combination tests. Scores were used to compare the two-ligand performance of candidates, while contact analysis helped generate structural explanations. The results of each round informed the design of the next.

We first included the fungal GPCRs involved in ascaroside sensing—Gpr2, Gpr3 and GprC—in the study, prepared predicted structures and ligands, and explored the pocket positions, key residues and docking methods described in the relevant literature. Receptor selection also considered the conditions for application in yeast. Because the plasma-membrane and mitochondria-associated localization of GprC reported in the literature could complicate heterologous localization and pathway integration, subsequent engineering focused on Gpr2, retained the study of Gpr3 and its mutants, and used GprC as a structural comparison.

Checks of the initial docking prompted us to correct two kinds of input problems. Inconsistent coordinate references between structures and search boxes could displace the actual search region from its intended position; loss of chemical information such as formal charges during format conversion could affect subsequent identification of charged contacts. These findings made input checking part of the analysis workflow.

We used structural superposition to unify the coordinate references of receptors, ligands and search boxes, and rechecked the search region against membrane orientation and the segments actually retained. Ligand files were checked by atom mapping to verify the correspondence of atoms, coordinates and structures between the original SDF and docking PDBQT. When PDBQT fully retained Meeko structural metadata, we recovered SDF files containing information such as docking poses and formal charges for contact analysis.

Subsequent mutant comparisons used consistent ligand inputs and search settings and retained original residue numbering so that scores and residue contacts could be traced together. Literature information established the research background and structural references; input checking verified computational conditions; two-ligand docking results were used to propose and screen engineering hypotheses.

## 1.2 Proposing initial engineering sites from contact frequencies and individual poses {#docking-sites toc="1.2 Proposing initial engineering sites from contact frequencies and individual poses"}

Initial site selection combined two scales of observation. First, we counted residue-contact frequencies across predicted receptor models and their docking poses, focusing on positions repeatedly involved in ligand contacts and comparing their occurrence for the two ligands. Second, we examined the specific contact differences between the two ligands in model_0, focusing on contacts detected only for one ligand among the analyzed poses and on their interaction types.

These two types of information are complementary. Statistics across models and poses describe the occurrence of contacts in the analyzed conformational set; comparisons of individual poses help locate differences and formulate questions that can be tested through side-chain substitutions. Interpretation of contact frequencies depends on the included models, poses and contact criteria, and their values describe this sampling result.

On this basis, we performed alanine substitutions around the candidate positions to examine how simplifying the side chain changed the scores and contacts of both ligands. The initial single sites included N84, L90, V110, R181, R345, W338 and V368. We generated the corresponding mutant sequences, predicted structures using AlphaFold 3, and then performed structural preparation, docking and contact comparisons with WT.

The initial results showed that N84A, V110A and V368A all had lower ascr#3 scores and higher ascr#18 scores under the structures and WT reference used at that time. This made N84, V110 and V368 the core positions for further side-chain comparisons; the score and contact changes at W338 and R181 also retained value for further exploration. Docking thereby converted the initial contact clues into testable substitution designs for the next round.

## 1.3 Expanding from a few substitutions to saturation scanning {#docking-saturation toc="1.3 Expanding from a few substitutions to saturation scanning"}

The alanine scan helped narrow the research scope while raising a new question: at the same position, could different side chains produce two-ligand changes in different directions?

Combining the scores and contact analysis from the earlier alanine scan with additional side-chain substitution results, we selected N84, V110, V368 and W338 for saturation scanning. Nineteen substitutions at each of four sites produced seventy-six single-mutant designs for systematic comparisons of how different side chains affect the scores and contact distributions of both ligands.

Saturation scanning showed that different substitutions at the same position could produce different score shifts. For example, N84V lowered the ascr#18 score and raised the ascr#3 score, whereas N84Y showed the opposite direction. Contact differences identify positions worth exploring; specific candidates still need to be determined from the two-ligand results after side-chain substitutions.

The main saturation-scan analysis used the lowest-scoring pose from each docking run and interpreted it together with its contact distribution. The seventy-six substitutions are different designs; the main result of each design comes from a single random seed, and neither multiple poses nor different substitutions are treated as independent experimental replicates. Original scores, structure sources and corresponding WT references are retained to allow the basis for candidate comparisons to be reviewed.

A negative ΔG means that the score for that ligand is lower than WT. Under this definition, a positive ΔΔG indicates a shift toward ascr#3 relative to WT, and a negative value indicates a shift toward ascr#18. This measure describes docking-score differences and is used for candidate comparisons; actual binding and selectivity require experimental assessment.

After determining the direction of the shift, we further examine the change in each score separately. A lower target-ligand score, a higher score for the other ligand, or both can all produce the same direction of ΔΔG, while corresponding to different candidate characteristics.

The first focused contact review after saturation scanning included N84V, V110C, V110M and V368R. All four used the same WT batch as a reference, with ascr#18 and ascr#3 scores of −6.492 and −6.625 kcal/mol, respectively.

Examining the two changes separately allows candidates to be chosen according to the research goal of separating the two ligands, and combinations to be designed on this basis.

## 1.5 Testing whether single-site changes can be retained together through combinations {#docking-combinations toc="1.5 Testing whether single-site changes can be retained together through combinations"}

The single-site results led to combination design: could substitutions with the same direction of shift enlarge the score difference? Could substitutions that lower the score of one ligand and raise the score of the other retain their respective behavior in the same receptor?

We constructed combinations according to candidate shift directions and patterns of score change, and compared them with both WT and each single-mutant parent. Comparisons with WT ask whether the original direction is retained; comparisons with the parents test whether a combination provides an additional benefit.

The combination of N84V and V110C provides a specific example. Both single mutants shift toward ascr#18, so we hoped that the combination would retain both N84V’s two-ligand separation and V110C’s improvement in the ascr#18 score. In the actual result, the combination had ΔG18 = −0.081 and ΔG3 = −0.411 kcal/mol, while ΔΔG changed to +0.330 kcal/mol: the ascr#3 score decreased more, and the original ascr#18 direction was not retained.

Further contact review showed that the ascr#18 pose in this combination still retained an R189 ionic-type contact and a W338 hydrophobic contact, but its score was 0.480 kcal/mol higher than that of V110C alone. This suggests that retention of some key contacts must be interpreted together with the complete pose and other contact changes, and that single-site score changes must be tested again through combination calculations.

Most tested combinations did not further improve the better parent’s target-ligand score or enlarge the two-ligand difference. This led subsequent work to continue focusing on single-site candidates, and clarified that combinations should be evaluated jointly against WT, each single-mutant parent and the complete contact network.

## 1.6 Converging from four focused candidates to two subjects for dynamic study {#docking-md-candidates toc="1.6 Converging from four focused candidates to two subjects for dynamic study"}

Combining score separation and contact review, we selected N84V and V368R from the four focused candidates for subsequent MD study. The two show opposite two-sided score changes, providing two representative candidates for further comparisons of the structural behavior of ascr#18 and ascr#3.

The contact analysis of N84V particularly illustrates the need to examine the surrounding residue network. In the selected docking pose, no direct ligand contact was detected at position 84 itself; relative to the same-batch WT, ascr#18 gained R189-related ionic and hydrogen-bond-type contacts and a W338 hydrophobic contact. These changes led us to focus interpretation on joint changes in ligand position and surrounding contacts, and to further ask whether these contacts can persist in a dynamic environment and whether they are related to ligand positioning.

Docking thereby advanced candidate screening into dynamic questions: in the membrane environment of the full-length receptor, does the ligand retain its initial position, which contacts persist, and which rearrange? We paired WT, N84V and V368R with the two ligands, respectively, to form six systems for MD comparisons. Subsequent dynamic analysis will test these structural clues and help revise our understanding of how the candidates act.

## 1.7 Extended analysis: seeking contact positions for parallel testing across receptors {#docking-cross-receptor toc="1.7 Extended analysis: seeking contact positions for parallel testing across receptors"}

Beyond the Gpr2 candidate study, we included Gpr3, GprC and the Caenorhabditis elegans receptors DAF-37 and SRBC-64 in the comparison to explore contact positions worth studying in parallel among functionally related receptors. Hu et al. compared ascaroside pockets through docking models of different receptors, providing a reference for this analysis. [Hu et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11222155/)

Five predicted models were used for each receptor. Within the same model, ascr#18 and ascr#3 contact frequencies were compared in pairs, and sequence alignment and structural mapping were then used to examine the correspondence of residue positions. Retention of the relevant residues in the actual constructs was recorded as well, keeping contact analysis, positional correspondence and the structures used consistent.

The TM6 tryptophans of the three fungal receptors—Gpr2 W338, Gpr3 W306 and GprC W388—show both ascr#18-favoring contact differences and support for sequence and structural positional correspondence. These results make them positions worth testing through parallel substitutions across receptors; substitutions at the corresponding positions can subsequently test whether their effects are similar.

This extension takes the site-selection method from two-ligand comparisons within a single receptor to cross-receptor analysis: contact differences identify candidate positions, sequence and structural correspondence assess comparability between positions, and construct records verify the objects actually analyzed. Original scores, per-pose contact tables, model identifiers, WT sources and structural-processing records jointly provide a basis for review, and allow other researchers to apply this analytical approach to their own receptor and ligand systems.
