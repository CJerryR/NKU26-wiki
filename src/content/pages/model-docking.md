## 1. From ligand contacts to receptor hypotheses {#docking-introduction toc="Research question"}

To build a sensing system that distinguishes ascr#18 from ascr#3, we seek receptor candidates with **complementary ligand preferences**. Molecular docking helps us identify residues contacted by each ligand, examine the effects of changing their side chains, and select candidates for further study.

We established **common comparison conditions**, identified positions from wild-type (WT) contact differences, and refined our designs through **alanine substitutions**, side-chain scans and combination tests. Scores compare the two-ligand profiles; contact analysis helps us propose structural explanations. Each round informed the next design.

We initially considered the fungal ascaroside-sensing GPCRs Gpr2, Gpr3 and GprC, prepared predicted structures and ligands, and consulted published pocket locations, key residues and docking methods. Receptor selection also considered application in yeast. Reported plasma-membrane and mitochondria-associated localization of GprC could complicate heterologous localization and pathway integration. We therefore focused engineering on Gpr2, retained Gpr3 and its mutants for study, and used GprC as a structural comparison.

Early checks exposed two input problems. Inconsistent coordinate references between structures and search boxes displaced the actual search region. Loss of chemical information, including formal charges, during format conversion affected the identification of charged contacts. Input checks became part of the analysis workflow.

We aligned structures to place receptors, ligands and search boxes in a common coordinate system, then checked the search region against membrane orientation and the retained receptor segments. **Atom mapping** linked the original SDF and docking PDBQT files, allowing us to check atoms, coordinates and structural correspondence. Where PDBQT files retained complete Meeko structural metadata, we recovered SDF files containing docking poses and formal charges for contact analysis.

Subsequent mutant comparisons used **consistent ligand inputs and search settings**, while retaining original residue numbering. Literature supplied biological and structural context; input checks established computational comparability; two-ligand docking generated engineering hypotheses.

Across predicted receptor models and docking poses, we counted residue contacts, looking for recurrent positions and differences between the ligands. Within `model_0`, we compared specific poses, including contacts detected for only one ligand and their interaction types.

In the initial structures and WT comparison, N84A, V110A and V368A all showed a lower score for ascr#3 and a higher score for ascr#18. N84, V110 and V368 became core sites for further side-chain comparisons. Score and contact changes at W338 and R181 also remained of interest. Docking thus converted initial contact differences into substitution hypotheses.

## 1.3 Extending the scan to all side chains {#docking-saturation toc="Saturation scan"}

The alanine scan narrowed the search and raised another question: can different side chains at the same position shift the two-ligand profile in different directions?

Combining alanine-scan scores, contact analysis and additional substitutions, we selected N84, V110, V368 and W338 for **saturation scanning**. Nineteen substitutions at each of four positions gave **76 single-mutant designs**.

Different substitutions at one site could produce opposite shifts. For example, N84V lowered the ascr#18 score and raised the ascr#3 score, whereas N84Y showed the reverse direction. Contact differences suggest positions to explore; candidate selection still requires the two-ligand results of individual substitutions.

The principal analysis used the lowest-scoring pose from each run and its contact distribution. These 76 substitutions are different designs. Each main result used **one random seed**; multiple poses and different substitutions were **not treated as independent replicates**. Original scores, structures and corresponding WT references were retained for review.

A negative $\Delta G$ means a lower score than WT for that ligand. Under this definition, a positive $\Delta\Delta G$ indicates a shift toward ascr#3 relative to WT; a negative value indicates a shift toward ascr#18. This is a docking-score comparison. Actual binding and selectivity require experimental evaluation.

We inspect the two changes separately: lowering the target-ligand score, raising the other-ligand score, or both can produce the same direction of $\Delta\Delta G$, while describing different candidate profiles.

The first focused contact review after saturation scanning included N84V, V110C, V110M and V368R. Their shared WT scores were −6.492 for ascr#18 and −6.625 for ascr#3, in kcal/mol.

N84V and V110C both shift toward ascr#18, but for different reasons. V110C lowers both scores, with the larger decrease for ascr#18. N84V lowers the ascr#18 score and raises the ascr#3 score. V110C therefore gives a larger improvement in the ascr#18 score, while N84V gives greater separation between the ligands.

V110M and V368R both shift toward ascr#3. For V110M, the ascr#3 score is close to WT, so the shift mainly reflects a higher ascr#18 score. V368R combines a lower ascr#3 score with a higher ascr#18 score. This distinction informed candidate and combination design.

## 1.5 Testing whether single-site effects combine {#docking-combinations toc="Combination tests"}

We asked whether substitutions with the same direction of shift could enlarge the score difference, and whether complementary single-site effects could coexist in one receptor. Combinations were **compared with WT and with each single-mutant parent**. The former tests whether the original direction is retained; the latter tests whether the combination adds an advantage.

N84V and V110C both shift toward ascr#18. We hoped their combination would combine N84V's two-ligand separation with V110C's lower ascr#18 score. Instead, the combination gave $\Delta G_{18}=-0.081$, $\Delta G_3=-0.411$ and $\Delta\Delta G=+0.330$ kcal/mol. The ascr#3 score decreased more, reversing the original ascr#18 direction.

The ascr#18 pose retained an R189 ionic-type contact and W338 hydrophobic contact, but its score was 0.480 kcal/mol higher than that of V110C alone. **Retaining selected contacts** therefore needs to be interpreted with the full pose and contact network; single-site effects must be tested again in combinations.

Most tested combinations did not further improve the better parent's target-ligand score or enlarge the two-ligand difference. We continued to focus on **single-site candidates** and evaluated combinations jointly against WT, their parents and the complete contact network.

## 1.6 Selecting two candidates for dynamics {#docking-md-candidates toc="Candidates for MD"}

Combining score separation and contact review, we selected **N84V and V368R** for MD. Their opposite, two-sided score changes provided representative candidates for comparing ascr#18 and ascr#3 behavior.

In the selected N84V pose, position 84 did not directly contact the ligand. Relative to the same-batch WT, ascr#18 gained R189-related ionic and hydrogen-bond-type contacts and a W338 hydrophobic contact. We therefore focused on changes in ligand position and neighboring contacts, asking whether these contacts persist dynamically and accompany ligand localization.

Docking led to dynamic questions: in the membrane environment of the full-length receptor, does the ligand retain its initial position, and which contacts persist or rearrange? WT, N84V and V368R were each paired with both ligands to form **six MD systems**. [Read the MD comparison](/model-md/).

## 1.7 Looking for comparable positions across receptors {#docking-cross-receptor toc="Cross-receptor analysis"}

Beyond Gpr2, we compared Gpr3, GprC and the *Caenorhabditis elegans* receptors DAF-37 and SRBC-64 to find contact positions worth studying in parallel. Hu et al. (2024) [2](#docking-reference-2) provided a reference for comparing ascaroside pockets across docking models.

For each receptor, five predicted models were used. Within a model, we paired ascr#18 and ascr#3 contact frequencies, then checked positional correspondence through **sequence alignment and structural mapping**. We also recorded which residues were retained in the actual constructs.

The fungal TM6 tryptophans **Gpr2 W338, Gpr3 W306 and GprC W388** combined ascr#18-biased contact differences with support from sequence and structural correspondence. They are candidates for parallel substitutions that could test whether corresponding positions have similar roles.

Contact differences nominate positions; sequence and structural correspondence establish comparability; construct records identify the structures actually analyzed. **Raw scores, per-pose contact tables, model numbers, WT sources and processing records** support review and reuse.
