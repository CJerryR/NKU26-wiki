    02Problem discovery

    A global story
    beneath our feet

    Soils everywhere are home to nematodes. Some of them feed on plant roots, and those can cost agriculture dearly.

    US$173 billion

    This is the estimated annual toll that plant-parasitic nematodes inflict on agriculture worldwide.

    Annual Review of Phytopathology

    Plant-parasitic nematodes are advancing across the globe. The time to detect, prevent, and control them is now.

    Distribution and Impact of Soybean Cyst Nematode in China

    Heterodera glycines, reported in 22 provincial-level regions

    It is estimated that soybean cyst nematode causes more than US$120 million in soybean yield losses in China every year.

    • 20–30%typical yield loss in infested soybean fields
    • 60–70%in severely infested fields; extreme cases approach total loss

    Soybean is the main host. Mung bean, pea, common bean and other food legumes can also be infected.

    • Province with published reports
    • Major soybean regions: Northeast and Huang-Huai-Hai

    A report does not mean the whole province is affected. National losses are not divided among provinces.

    Reported Distribution and Major Hosts of Southern Root-knot Nematode in China

    Meloidogyne incognita, recorded in 20 provinces, plus detections in Xinjiang

    Southern root-knot nematode has an extremely broad host range. It can infect vegetables, field crops, cash crops and many medicinal plants: more than 3,000 plant species in all.

    • Tomato
    • Cucumber
    • Pepper
    • Eggplant
    Hunan vegetable survey

    About 80% disease incidence in greenhouse vegetables against about 10% in open fields; M. incognita made up 87% of the root-knot populations identified. These are incidence and species shares, not yield losses.

    • Province record (CABI)
    • Detected in protected vegetables, 2021–2023 survey

    Records show where the species has been reported, not that whole provinces are heavily affected. Rice root-knot damage in China mostly involves another species, M. graminicola, which is kept separate.

    How do nematodes infect plants?

    03Scientific clue

    The hidden threat

    A hidden activity beneath the soil.

    1. 1Searching for a host

      J2 juveniles move through the soil, searching for host roots.

    2. 2Releasing ascarosides

      As nematodes move and live, small molecules called ascarosides are produced and released into the soil. We follow two of them: ascr#3 and ascr#18.

    3. 3Invading the roots

      Once it finds a host, the nematode enters the root and sets up a feeding relationship. The root starts to lose its normal structure and function.

    4. 4Symptoms above ground

      Damaged roots take up less water and fewer nutrients. By the time symptoms show above ground, the infection below may already be well under way.

    Nematodes leave chemical traces.

    The nematode itself

    Find the organism.

    Its DNA

    Read its genetic identity.

    Its chemical signals

    Sense what it leaves in the soil.

    Ascarosides take part in nematode development and behaviour, and in communication within and between species. Instead of looking for the nematode itself or reading its DNA, we try to sense the chemical-ecological signals it leaves in the environment: a route to nematode risk sensing that complements nucleic-acid testing.

    ascr#3sugar ring and a short, unsaturated side chain

    ascr#18the same ring with a longer, saturated side chain

    Project hypothesis

    Could a combination of signals tell us more?

    Free-living and plant-parasitic nematodes may have different ascaroside profiles. We focus on ascr#3, ascr#18 and how they combine, to explore whether these signals could help tell background nematode activity apart from plant-parasitic risk.

    A single ascaroside may only tell us that a related signal is present.

    If these molecules are clues nematodes leave behind, how could we turn them into a result people can read directly?

    04Our solution

    Turning chemical signals into color.

    We translate the chemical signals nematodes leave behind into a color people can read by eye.

    The receptor recognizes. Engineered yeast relays and amplifies. Betaxanthin makes the result visible.

    1. Recognize

      A receptor of fungal origin, placed on the membrane of engineered yeast, recognizes the ascarosides we focus on.

    2. Relay and amplify

      The yeast's own coupled signalling pathway carries the signal inside and amplifies it.

    3. Make it visible

      The amplified signal switches on betaxanthin synthesis, a yellow pigment that can be seen by eye.

    Protein names, promoters and constructs are documented on the Design and Engineering pages. A color response still needs validation before it could support on-site risk pre-screening; it is not a diagnosis.

    04How the work connects

    A loop, not a line.

    We did not build a sensor first and look for a use later. Real settings raise the questions, the wet lab produces evidence, the dry lab helps explain and predict, and results go back to the lab and the field to be tested again.

    1. Wet lab

      Can we see it?

      Whether the receptor works in engineered yeast, whether the system responds to the target ascarosides, whether betaxanthin gives a readable color, and whether background, non-target signals or soil interfere.

    2. Dry lab

      Can we explain it?

      Models linking ascaroside input to color output, the parameters that matter most, what ascr#3 and ascr#18 together might add, and how noise and degradation affect reliability.

    3. Human Practices, product design and the Eco model

      Can it inform decisions?

      Who needs early risk information and when, how sampling can be simplified, how results route uncertain samples to professional testing, and how biosafety is built in.

    Reading times, thresholds, combination rules and uncertainty will be filled in from the final models and experiments.

    All relationships as a list
    • Soil plan provides data to the dry lab and samples to the wet lab.
    • Society takes part in the soil plan and responds to questionnaires.
    • Field data calibrate the Eco model; questionnaires support it; the Eco model makes predictions for society.
    • Education and outreach feed science back to society.
    • Society shapes the project; the project is applied back to society.
    • The signalling ODE model helps optimize the project; expert interviews guide it.
    • The wet lab asks experts questions; soil samples are tested in the wet lab.
    • Wet-lab measurements feed the ODE model; the model returns simulations and analysis to the wet lab.
    • Docking and molecular dynamics suggest candidate receptor variants for the wet lab.

    06Closing

    Make the next step better informed.

    In the opening, color meant the detective had found a clue. Here it means a user has a first piece of risk information to act on.

    1. Soil sampling

      Collect representative soil at the right time and place.

    2. Risk pre-screening

      The field detective senses nematode-related chemical signals and gives an initial, on-site risk prompt.

    3. Professional confirmation

      Samples with a higher-risk or uncertain result go to professional testing to confirm species, population density and other details.

    4. Management decision

      Professional results, the crop, the region and the planting plan together shape a targeted management plan.

    We hope to turn the invisible chemical clues left by nematode activity into a risk signal that can be seen, understood and acted on before planting.

    Color is not a sign that the case is closed. It is a prompt to keep observing, to test professionally, or to begin targeted management.

    • For growers

      Notice nematode risk earlier, instead of only after symptoms appear above ground.

    • For the testing system

      Send high-risk and uncertain samples to professional testing with better reason, linking on-site screening, confirmation and management.

    • For the technology

      Explore how nematode chemical-ecological signals and engineered biosensors could combine into a new way of sensing risk in the field.
